CC3. Debate, use a visualisation in policy commentary

Policy Topic: Does increasing NHS funding automatically lead to better performance and shorter waiting times in emergency departments?

The NHS healthcare expenditure data (2003–2024) shows a consistent upward trend in funding, reaching record highs in recent years.

In contrast, A&E waiting times (over 4 hours) have risen sharply over the same period, particularly spiking after 2010.

Despite consistent funding increases, waiting times have worsened, suggesting structural inefficiencies (not cash shortages) are driving poor performance.

CC4. Replication. Recreate, then improve, someone else’s chart

I replicated a Guardian chart comparing poverty in the US and China. The original used separate scales to suggest that poverty reduction in the US failed compared to China, due to policy choices.

The original comparison was misleading because it ignored the starting baselines. By merging the data onto a single shared axis, I revealed the true context: US poverty has remained historically low, whereas China’s "success" was a reduction from extreme poverty levels. This correction challenges the article's narrative by demonstrating that the two nations operate on vastly different economic scales and political regimes, making a direct "policy choice" comparison statistically flawed.

CC5. API and Scraper

I used the World Bank API to track remittance inflows to Mexico, India, and the Philippines (1974-2024). This chart highlights the massive growth in money flowing to the Global South over the last 50 years.

API Description

I used this base URL: https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/

For the parameters, I included the country codes (MX;IN;PH), the specific indicator ID for personal remittances, and the format parameter.

CC7. Maps

I created a coordinates map of Wales to visualise environmental permits for installation and waste operations. The bubble sizes correspond to the scale of the facility, highlighting clusters of industrial and waste management activity across the region.

For Scotland, I produced a choropleth map displaying Gross Disposable Household Income (GDHI) per household. The color intensity reveals regional economic disparities, with darker areas indicating higher average disposable income.

CC8. Big Data

Using Google Colab, I analysed the Long Run Prices Database (LRPD) set out in Davies (2021). I aggregated monthly price data from 1988 to 2025 to trace the cost of traditional proteins (beef, chicken, eggs) across supermarkets and smaller stores.

This second chart applies the same methodology to alternative protein sources, specifically beans, peas, and peanut butter. By isolating these plant-based staples over the same 37-year period, we can compare their long-term price stability against the volatility seen in the meat market.

CC9. Interactive charts

For this chart, I visualised regional income inequality across the UK, incorporating both a year slider and a dropdown menu. These tools allow to compare the whole UK against London to reveal disparities over time and location.

This chart tracks Child Benefit coverage in the UK from 2003 to 2024. A timeline slider allows to observe the year-by-year evolution of coverage rates, showing shifts in social support policy over the last two decades.
