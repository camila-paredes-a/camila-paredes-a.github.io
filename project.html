Income, Housing Costs, and Family Wellbeing in the UK

This project analyses how income, housing costs, and family-related benefits interact to shape the living standards of households with children in the UK. The analysis focuses on residual income after housing, a measure that reflects material wellbeing and financial resilience.

The project pays particular attention to lower-middle income families, who often earn too much to qualify for full means-tested support but remain highly exposed to housing costs and benefit withdrawal. By combining evidence on housing expenditure, affordability, residual income, regional variation, and Child Benefit coverage, the project identifies a “squeeze of the middle”.

This chart quantifies the additional weekly housing costs faced by households after having children, comparing families with and without children across the income distribution. Parenthood is associated with systematically higher housing expenditure, reflecting the need for larger dwellings, family-friendly locations, and more stable tenure.

This chart shows housing costs as a share of total household spending before and after having children. Housing absorbs a larger proportion of the household budget for families, especially at lower income levels.

This chart examines how much income households retain after paying for housing. Residual income varies sharply across income groups, revealing that gross earnings alone provide a misleading picture of living standards.

For families with children, housing costs significantly compress disposable resources, particularly among lower-middle income households.

This chart introduces regional variation into the analysis by comparing residual income after housing across UK regions. By holding the income concept constant and allowing geography to vary, the chart isolates the role of regional housing markets in shaping family wellbeing.

The results show that families with similar gross incomes can experience markedly different living standards depending on where they live. Regions with higher housing costs consistently exhibit lower residual income, even when earnings are comparable.

From a policy perspective, this highlights the limits of nationally uniform benefit levels. When housing costs vary substantially across regions, flat-rate support fails to equalise living standards, exposing families in high-cost areas to greater financial strain.

This chart examines the coverage of Child Benefit among families with children, focusing on who receives support, evaluating policy reach.

The data reveals that a non-trivial share of families who could benefit from support are either partially covered or excluded altogether. These gaps are particularly relevant for lower-middle income households, who may earn above eligibility thresholds for some programmes but still face high housing costs.

This final chart presents a regression-based analysis linking residual income after housing to gross income, housing costs, and benefit receipt for families with children. This approach estimates which factors exert the strongest influence on household living standards.

The results indicate that housing costs have a large and persistent negative association with residual income, while increases in gross income generate diminishing gains once benefits begin to taper. For lower-middle income households, higher earnings do not translate proportionally into improved living standards.

Conclusion

These graphs suggest that financial pressure among families with children in the UK is driven less by low earnings alone and more by the interaction between housing costs and welfare design. For lower-middle income households in particular, earning more does not necessarily translate into higher living standards, providing clear empirical evidence of the squeeze of the middle.

From a policy perspective, the results suggest that income-based support alone is insufficient. Flat national benefits and sharp eligibility thresholds fail to reflect regional housing pressures and leave many families exposed. More effective policy responses would require better coordination between housing and welfare systems, including housing-cost-adjusted benefits or targeted support for families just above traditional eligibility cut-offs.
